Findings

Fan debates, checked against the data

Coaches, players and fans argue about these every season, on forums, in coaching blogs and in the stands. We tested each one against 4.45 million rallies of Division I play-by-play.

    Fan debate

    Do timeouts stop runs? No

    Coaches call timeouts to cut off the other team's run. We took every timeout the scorers logged that was called by the team being scored on, and compared the next rally with the same moments when nobody called one. Both are measured against how often that team won rallies on serve receive in that match, so team strength cancels out.

    Fan debate

    Is momentum real? Mostly no

    When a team reels off points on its serve, the other team does win less often. The question is why. We replayed every 2024 and 2025 set thousands of times with no momentum at all, where each rally is independent, and compared the streaks.

    What the replay shows

    With no momentum and no day-to-day form, long runs barely change the odds. Add match-day form (good teams having good nights) and the simulated decline matches the real one within a fraction of a point for runs of one to five.

    So most streaks come from a team playing well that night, not from the streak itself. After six or more straight points, real teams do slightly worse than the replay. That could be a small late-run momentum effect, or form that varies more than the replay assumes.

    Fan debate

    Serve tough or serve it in? Neither decides it

    The usual coaching target is one ace for every missed serve. Top teams don't come close, and they don't serve any differently from everyone else. Their edge comes after the pass.

    Fan debate

    How much is home court worth?

    Put two evenly matched teams on the floor. At a neutral site, each wins half the time. Move the match to one team's gym and the home team wins of the time. The edge is small on any single rally and adds up over a match.

    Under the hood

    From rallies to a win probability

    This page walks through the whole project in four steps: the data we started with, what the model learns from it, how the model turns two teams into a win probability, and how we checked that the predictions hold up.

    1. The data

      Every touch of every Division I match since 2020.

    2. What the model learns

      Twelve numbers per team, one season at a time.

    3. Calculating a match

      Exact odds built up from the rally, with no dice rolls.

    4. Testing

      Predicting unseen matches, then comparing with what happened.

    Step 1 · The data

    Every touch of every match

    We started with the official play-by-play from stats.ncaa.org for every Division I women's match, , collected by the open-source ncaavolleyballr project. Each row is one touch: who touched the ball, for which team, what kind of touch it was, and the score after it.

    matches
    rallies
    99.94%of rebuilt match results agree with the official box score

    One real rally, as recorded

    Each card is one possession: a new one starts every time the ball crosses the net. These are the rows the model learns from.

    What we fixed, and what's missing

    • Rallies are rebuilt from changes in the score. Rows recorded during TV timeouts have no score, and error rows credit the wrong team. Both are corrected.
    • About 1,700 matches listed twice in the 2025 files are removed.
    • Not recorded: how good each touch was. A perfect pass and a shanked one look the same. Where the ball went, and block touches that stay in play, are missing too.
    • Not recorded: lineups and injuries. The model sees teams, not players.
    Step 2 · What the model learns

    Twelve numbers per team

    From those touches the model learns how good each team is at every step of a rally: serving, passing, its first attack after the pass, defending the other team's first attack, and the scramble after a dig, on offense and on defense. Each step gets two numbers, twelve in all, for every team in every season.

    Turning rallies into those numbers takes three moves: count what each team did at each step, adjust for who it played, and store the result. Here is one of Kentucky's twelve, worked through.

      The 12 numbers behind

      "Counted" is what happened in the team's own matches. "Adjusted" is the model's estimate against an average opponent, after accounting for schedule; teams with few matches also lean on the season before. Bold adjusted rates beat the league average. Showing Team A from the Matchups page.

      Step 3 · Calculating a match

      From two teams to a win probability

      To predict a match, the model combines the two teams' numbers one step at a time. Nothing here is simulated. Instead of rolling dice to play thousands of pretend matches, the model adds up the probability of every possible rally and every possible score. That gives the answer a simulation would settle on, without the randomness. Below is the full calculation for the matchup chosen on the Matchups page.

      Good and bad nights: match-day form

      A team's twelve numbers describe its season average, but no team plays exactly to its average every night. Travel, an injury, a hot hitter or a loud gym can lift a team or flatten it for a whole match. The model can't see those causes, so it allows for a small random swing in each match: one team may be a little sharper than usual and the other a little flatter. Since nobody knows in advance which way the swing will go, the model averages the odds over every possible swing.

      A typical swing moves a team's sideout rate by about percentage points, for example from 57% to . The size comes from the data: when one team plays above its expected level in a match, its opponent tends to play below theirs by a matching amount.

      The swing pulls lopsided odds a little toward 50%. That matters most for long shots: a team that would almost never win if both sides played to their averages still wins now and then when it has a great night and its opponent has a flat one.

      Why small rally edges matter

      The last step uses nothing but the scoring rules: every rally scores, the winner serves next, sets go to 25 (the fifth to 15), win by 2. Set the two rally numbers yourself to see how a small gap grows over a match. The Division I average is about 57%.

      Step 4 · Testing

      Predicting matches the model hadn't seen

      A model can describe the past perfectly and still predict badly. So every prediction on this site was made using only matches played before it.

      1. Week by week

        Through the 2025 season the model was refit every week on matches already played, then asked to predict the next week. The model's settings, such as how much to trust last season, were chosen using 2024, so 2025 is a clean test.

      2. Brackets

        For each NCAA tournament from 2021 to 2025, the model saw only matches before the first round, then computed every team's odds over every possible path through the bracket, with home court for the host schools.

      3. Good and bad nights

        Teams play better or worse on a given night. Match odds average over a small random swing in form for each match, which keeps the odds for long shots honest.

      What was simulated

      Predictions are calculated, not simulated. Simulation was used for one thing: testing momentum. We replayed every 2024 and 2025 set many times with each rally independent of the last, and compared the streaks with real ones. That result is on the Findings page.
